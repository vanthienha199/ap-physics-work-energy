Five ideas you need before learning “work.” The quiz uses them all the time.

The net force is all the forces added together (forces in opposite directions subtract).

Work: does the force help the object move?

Work measures how much energy a force puts into (or takes out of) an object while it moves.

W = F · d · cos θ

F is the size of the force, d is the distance moved, and θ is the angle between the force and the direction of motion. Unit: joule (J) = N·m.

cos θ does one job: it keeps only the part of the force that points along the motion. That part is F∥ = F·cos θ. The perpendicular part does nothing.

Trap in Q1 and Q2: “It moves at constant speed, so the work is zero” is wrong. Each force still does work. The student’s positive work and friction’s negative work just add up to zero.

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

Work is energy going in or out. So where does it go? Mostly into kinetic energy.

Kinetic energy is the energy of a moving object:

K = ½ m v²

An object at rest has K = 0. Twice as fast means four times the K.

The work–energy theorem:

Wnet = ΔK

Add up the work of all the forces, and you get how much the kinetic energy changes. Positive net work: the object speeds up. Negative: it slows down. Zero: same speed.

Q3 (crane): tension is 400 N up, gravity is 300 N down, so the net force is 100 N up. Wnet = 100 × 2 = +200 J into the box.

Q15: W = 8 × 4 = 32 J = ½(1)v², so v = 8 m/s.

Conservative and non-conservative forces

Potential energy, thermal energy, and why gravity doesn’t care which path you take.

Gravitational potential energy is energy “stored” by being high up: U = m·g·h. When the object falls, it is “released” as kinetic energy.

Conservative forceNon-conservative force
ExamplesGravity, spring forceFriction, air resistance
Does the work depend on the path?No (path-independent). Only the height change matters.Yes (path-dependent). The longer the path, the more is lost.
Where does the energy go?Stored as potential energy and can be gotten backTurns into thermal energy (things get warmer) and can’t be gotten back

Force vs. position graphs: work = area

When the force changes, you can’t just multiply F × d. Instead: work = area under the F–x graph.

Why? For a constant force, the graph is a rectangle F tall and d wide. Its area = F × d, which is exactly the work formula.

  • Rectangle: length × width
  • Triangle: ½ × base × height
  • Below the x-axis: negative work, so subtract it

Q8: triangle ½ × 4 × 20 = 40 J.

Q9: +40 (above the axis) − 30 (below the axis) = 10 J.

Power: how fast work is done

Two people carry the same box up one floor. The one who does it in 5 seconds has more power than the one who takes 50 seconds.

P = W / t = F · v

Unit: watt (W) = J/s. Use W/t when you know the work and the time (average power). Use F·v when you know the force and the velocity at one moment (instantaneous power).

If the force points opposite to the motion, P is negative: the force is taking energy out.

Q10: constant speed, so the pedaling force = 60 N. P = 60 × 5 = 300 W.

Q11: A: 100 N × 10 m / 5 s = 200 W. B: 50 N × 20 m / 5 s = 200 W. Equal.

Q15: v = 8 m/s (from part 5), so P = 8 N × 8 m/s = 64 W.

Try it: P = F · v and its sign

Q16: the elevator, the hardest question

A 1000-kg elevator, v = 5 m/s, acceleration 2 m/s². Three steps:

Step 1: which way is the acceleration? Speeding up: acceleration points the same way as the motion. Slowing down: acceleration points opposite to the motion.

Step 2: find the tension T with Fnet = ma. Acceleration up: T = m(g + a). Acceleration down: T = m(g − a).

Step 3: P = T · v, negative if T (always up) is opposite to the motion.

Trap (answer A): using T = mg = 10 000 N gives −50 000 W, and using m(g − a) = 8 000 N gives −40 000 W. Moving down while slowing down means the acceleration is up, so T = 12 000 N and P = −60 000 W → answer B.
